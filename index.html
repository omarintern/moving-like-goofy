Abstract

Video models can draw Goofy, but they move him like everyone else: the motion comes from what they know about people in general. The way he carries himself, the hunch, the bent knees, the loose stride, lives only in his films, the Disney shorts of the 1940s. This project takes it from there: his way of moving learned from 71 short clips, 195 seconds in all, in 103 minutes of training on one GPU, and his posture one fixed pose, set by hand.

The model builds on STyMo, and the split of a style into a posture and a way of moving through it is STyMo's; STyMo learns from motion capture. What this project adds: the character's manner learned from his films instead; the extraction and checks that make old film usable as training data, reading his pose off drawings that slide and cheat the camera, marking what is usable frame by frame, measuring his travel against fixed objects in the shot and leaving travel out where there are none; a cleanup that keeps him on the person's path and footing, every planted foot at the person's own spot, nothing through the floor; and two uses after the motion, a video model painting him into the original shot and a humanoid robot. Here the posture is one fixed pose, set by hand, and only the way of moving is learned from the example clips.

Method Overview

The model builds on STyMo (Ponton, Winkler, Kavan, Ye and Kadlecek, SIGGRAPH 2026): the split of a style into two parts, a posture and a way of moving through it, is STyMo's. Here the posture is not learned. It is one fixed pose: his posture offsets at the legs, spine, neck and head, frozen into a single rest pose by hand and laid onto every input the same way before the model, with the knee bend fading as the input's own knees bend; at run time the average of what posture remains across the example clips is added on top. The way of moving is the learned part: the residual from the person's motion to his, over the example clips. Around STyMo this project adds the character's manner learned from his films rather than from motion capture; the extraction and checks that make old film usable as training data, his travel measured against fixed objects in the shot and left out where there are none; the final pass that keeps him on the person's path and footing; and the two uses after the motion, the video model and the robot.

From his films to a fit. A fit is the 3D pose of a body read off the film, frame by frame, on a skeleton of 22 joints; each of his clips is fitted, and what is usable is marked by eye, so only the passes go further. A neutral for each clip: a person doing the same action plainly, on their own clock, whether generated by a motion model, recorded with motion capture, or filmed and fitted. The pair is his clip and its neutral aligned in time, with his fit corrected only where it breaks the line of the action, never his manner. Every input, his and the person's, is built one way: the bearing, the fixed pose laid onto the body, then the knee saturation, which fades its knee bend as the person's own knees bend, so a deep bend is not bent further. The dataset is 71 pairs with 4 kept out for testing, and the model is a small transformer that learns the residual: the difference from the person's motion to his, over windows of 16 frames.

At run time a new original input is prepared the same way, and the operator, the step that lays the residual onto it, produces the Goofy-style output. Then one solve, a single pass that settles everything at once, brings the result as close to the model's output as the rules allow: every stance, a foot held on the floor, kept at the original input's own foot spot; held hands kept on their grips; nothing under the floor; every joint inside his ranges; no point changing speed faster than his films do; the body on the original input's path. Where the rules cannot all hold, the conflict is reported, never traded quietly. The checks run on every output and are reported to a reviewer, never acted on by themselves. The output is a pose for every frame, as joint rotations: a video model renders him into the shot from it, and a robot takes it directly.

Cartoon footage is hard to fit. The camera follows him, his drawn feet slide on the painted ground, and a pose reader trained on people folds a leg the wrong way or swaps his legs. We mark when each foot is down and where the leg joints are on a few drawings, train models to mark the rest, and refit his legs with his own bone lengths, starting from the human reading. How far he travels is measured against a fixed object in the scene; where there is none, travel is left out of training.

How it is made

A later stage

Today's example clips put his clip beside a person's performance of the same action, aligned in time. The two are not one to one: a different step, a different hand, and the model learns that difference as style. A later stage builds the plain version out of his own clip, pushing it toward ordinary human motion with a model of how people move while keeping what the action is, and chooses per clip which aspects are made plain and which are kept. In a first probe on three clips the difference the model has to learn fell from about 30 degrees per joint to 6, with his kick height and crouch depth landing in that difference rather than in the plain version.
