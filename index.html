Abstract

Video models can draw Goofy, but they move him like everyone else: the motion comes from what they know about people in general. The way he carries himself, the hunch, the bent knees, the loose stride, lives only in his films, the Disney shorts of the 1940s and early 1950s. This project takes it from there: his way of moving learned from 71 short clips, 195 seconds in all, in 103 minutes of training on one GPU, and his posture one fixed pose, measured from his fits and frozen by hand.

The model builds on STyMo, and the split of a style into a posture and a way of moving through it is STyMo's; STyMo learns from motion capture. What this project adds: the character's manner learned from his films instead; the extraction and checks that make old film usable as training data, reading his pose off drawings that slide and cheat the camera, marking what is usable frame by frame, leaving his travel out of training where it can't be trusted; a cleanup that keeps him on the person's path and footing, every planted foot at the person's own spot, nothing through the floor; and two uses after the motion, a video model painting him into the original shot and a humanoid robot. Here the posture is one fixed pose, measured from his fits and frozen by hand, and only the way of moving is learned from the example clips.

Method Overview

The model builds on STyMo (Ponton, Winkler, Kavan, Ye and Kadlecek, SIGGRAPH 2026): the split of a style into two parts, a posture and a way of moving through it, is STyMo's. Here the posture is not learned. It is one fixed pose: his posture offsets at the legs, spine, neck and head, measured from his fits and frozen into a single rest pose by hand, then laid onto the plain performance before the model, at half strength for inputs read off video, with the knee bend fading as the input's own knees bend; at run time the average of what posture remains across the example clips is added on top. His own clips keep their own posture. The way of moving is the learned part: the residual from the person's motion to his, over the example clips. Around STyMo this project adds the character's manner learned from his films rather than from motion capture; two additions inside the model, a phase label on every frame and a foot-contact term in training; the extraction and checks that make old film usable as training data, his travel left out of training where it can't be trusted; the final pass that keeps him on the person's path and footing; and the two uses after the motion, the video model and the robot.

His clips come from 45 of his films, where scene detection found 3,812 scenes and shots. 1,384 were removed by rule with no one watching, for running under 2 or 3 seconds or having no Goofy in the frame, and 1,771 were never judged. The other 657, about 80 minutes, were judged by hand, and 64 were kept, giving 71 training pairs and four held out to check the model. Of those dropped after judging, 28 were removed by a curation rule, not by eye. The rule required a complete action that a plain verb can name (at least two full cycles if it repeats, the whole arc if not) and a fit that reproduces the cartoon.

From his films to a fit. A fit is the 3D pose of a body read off the film, frame by frame, on a skeleton of 22 joints; each of his clips is fitted, and what is usable is marked by eye, so only the passes go further. A neutral for each clip: a plain performance of the same action, on its own clock, generated by a motion model from a one-line description, Kimodo for most and HY-Motion for the rest, and checked by eye. The pair is his clip and its neutral aligned in time, with his fit corrected only where it breaks the line of the action, never his manner. Every plain performance, in training and at run time, is built one way: the bearing, the fixed pose laid onto the body, then the knee saturation, which fades its knee bend as the person's own knees bend, so a deep bend is not bent further. The dataset is 71 pairs with 4 kept out for testing, and the model is a small transformer that learns the residual: the difference from the person's motion to his, over windows of 32 frames, about a second around each frame.

Two additions to STyMo's model come with it. First, every moment of the person's motion carries a phase label: MoMask's pretrained motion tokenizer (Guo et al., CVPR 2024), trained on HumanML3D (Guo et al., CVPR 2022), turns each fifth of a second into a code, and the codes are grouped into ten kinds of movement, such as a step, a turn or a kick. The residual sees the phase now and through the next second. The labels describe the person's motion, never his. STyMo uses phases only to line up its training pairs; no label reaches its model. Second, training penalizes a planted foot that lifts, sinks or slides. STyMo takes foot contacts as an input and fixes the feet after the model runs. The term was kept after a side-by-side test judged by eye, and his kick kept its height. Two of STyMo's parts are changed rather than added. The posture added at run time is the example clips' average, where STyMo picks one with a classifier. And STyMo's gate, one learned score per frame for the whole body, becomes a check of each limb against the plain performances the model trained on: where a limb is unfamiliar, the model's change on it fades, leaving it as the prepared input has it, his fixed pose included.

At run time a new original input is prepared the same way, with the fixed pose at half strength if it was read off video, and the operator, the step that lays the residual onto it, produces the Goofy-style output. Then a head rule sets his neck and head to the person's gaze as performed in the scene, in place of the model's own head; the back can hunch while the head stays up. Then one solve settles everything together: it makes up to six passes and keeps the one with the fewest conflicts. It brings the result as close to the model's output as the rules allow: every stance, a foot held on the floor, kept at the original input's own foot spot; held hands kept on their grips; nothing under the floor; every joint inside his ranges; no point changing speed faster than his films do; the body on the original input's path. Where the rules cannot all hold, the conflict is reported, never traded quietly. The checks run on every output and are reported to a reviewer, never acted on by themselves. The output is a pose for every frame, as joint rotations: a video model renders him into the shot from it, and a robot takes it directly.

Cartoon footage is hard to fit. The camera follows him, his drawn feet slide on the painted ground, and a pose reader trained on people folds a leg the wrong way or swaps his legs. Most of his clips are read with FlowHMR or GVHMR, pose readers trained on people, and corrected where they break. His leg joints were marked by hand on 764 drawings, and in ten clips his legs were refit with his own bone lengths. When each foot is down was marked by hand on 1,206 frames of 19 clips, and set by rule elsewhere. His travel comes from a fixed object in the scene where there is one, otherwise from his planted steps or the pose reader's own estimate, and is left out of training where it can't be trusted.

How it is made

What's next

What the data holds. Every moment of the training pairs carries one of ten phases, read from the person's plain performance, not from him, so standing means the person stays in place, whatever he does meanwhile. The mix is lopsided. Three phases hold about 70 percent of the frames: standing and slow moves at 29 percent, arm moves such as a wave or a stretch at 24, and sitting, squatting and crawling at 17. The other seven, among them turns, steps, walks, runs, jumps and kicks, appear mostly as slivers, a fifth of a second at the edge of some other move. Kicks are among the training clips, but a kick lasts a fraction of a second, so most of a kick clip's frames fall in other phases. His manner shows most clearly there so far, in the golf swing's settle after the strike, which comes while the person stays put. We think that is because standing is where his data is richest. That is untested; more clips in the thin phases would test it.

Two ways to grow it. The first is more of his film: the models trained on the hand-marked drawings mark new footage, with the eye checking samples, aimed at the thin phases, each new clip holding the move itself, such as whole walk cycles rather than a step at the edge. The second is generating more of him: a model that learns what style is across many styles, from actors performing them in motion capture, then set to his from a few clips, to make new performances where the data is thin. That gives control over the mix, but his particulars still come only from his clips: it could carry his bearing to new actions, but not invent gags he never did, and every result is still judged by eye.

Finer phases. More clips let the phases split finer, since each needs at least three clips to be used. But the ten were grouped once from the tokenizer's codes over thousands of other people's motions, and in that grouping sitting never came apart from squatting at any count tried, so a walk may not come apart from a run however many clips are added. Two untested alternatives: group the codes on this project's own data, or label the person's motion by what it means rather than how it moves, with a vision model, about to set off rather than standing, so the pairs can supply his wind-up for that moment.

Better pairs. Today's example clips put his clip beside a generated plain performance of the same action, aligned in time. The two are not one to one: a different step, a different hand, and the model learns that difference as style. A later stage builds the plain version out of his own clip, pushing it toward ordinary human motion with a model of how people move while keeping what the action is, and chooses per clip which aspects are made plain and which are kept. In a first probe on three clips the difference the model has to learn fell from about 30 degrees per joint to 6, with his kick height and crouch depth landing in that difference rather than in the plain version.

His fits: FlowHMR (Wang et al., "FlowHMR: Physically Plausible Motion Capture from Video", 2026) and GVHMR (Shen et al., "World-Grounded Human Motion Recovery via Gravity-View Coordinates", SIGGRAPH Asia 2024), on the SMPL-X body model (Pavlakos et al., "Expressive Body Capture: 3D Hands, Face, and Body from a Single Image", CVPR 2019).
The neutrals: Kimodo (Rempe et al., NVIDIA, "Kimodo: Scaling Controllable Human Motion Generation", 2026) and HY-Motion 1.0 (Tencent Hunyuan, "HY-Motion 1.0: Scaling Flow Matching Models for Text-To-Motion Generation", 2025).
The phase labels: MoMask (Guo et al., "MoMask: Generative Masked Modeling of 3D Human Motions", CVPR 2024), trained on HumanML3D (Guo et al., "Generating Diverse and Natural 3D Human Motions from Text", CVPR 2022), whose motions come from AMASS.
New inputs: the shot put from AMASS's Eyes Japan set (AMASS: Mahmood et al., "AMASS: Archive of Motion Capture as Surface Shapes", ICCV 2019; Eyes Japan: mocapdata.com, Eyes, JAPAN Co. Ltd.); the ballet from HY-Motion 1.0; the person videos for the golf swing, bus stop, kick and skid generated with Seedance 1.5 pro (ByteDance) and read with GVHMR.
The model builds on STyMo: Ponton, Winkler, Kavan, Ye and Kadlecek, "STyMo: Fast and Controllable Few-Shot Motion Style Transfer", SIGGRAPH 2026 (project page).
The video model: Wan 2.7 (Alibaba).
The robot: the Unitree G1, under NVIDIA's GEAR-SONIC balance controller (Luo et al., "SONIC: Supersizing motion tracking for natural humanoid whole-body control", Science Robotics 2026), the motions mapped onto it with GMR (Araujo et al., "Retargeting Matters: General Motion Retargeting for Humanoid Motion Tracking", 2025), simulated in MuJoCo.
